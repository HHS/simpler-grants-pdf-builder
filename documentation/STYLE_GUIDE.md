## How to use this guide

Refer to this guide to:

- Address specific NOFO writing and editing issues.
- Support NOFO design needs.
- Reduce decision fatigue over formatting and style quirks.
- Reiterate key points from our default style guides for consistency and readability.

## Accessibility

### Images and alt text

Most NOFOs do not include images other than optional cover photographs.

- Use only images that serve a purpose. We recommend avoiding images that are merely decorative.
- All images must have an alternative text description, or alt text. See [How to add alt text in Microsoft Word](https://it.umn.edu/services-technologies/how-tos/microsoft-word-add-edit-alt-text) (Source: University of Minnesota).
- Read more about [Authoring Meaningful Alternative Text](https://www.section508.gov/create/alternative-text/).

## Formatting

### Bold, italics, and other formatting

#### Bold

- Use bold to indicate emphasis.
- Limit bolded text to important information; do not overuse it.

Note that formatting alone does not create a heading. Instead, use structured (styled) headings. See [headings](#headings).

**Table: Using bold for emphasis**

| Items to bold | When to do this |
| --- | --- |
| Scoring criteria point values, like: **(5 points)** | When they run into a line in text or tables (not a heading) |
| The following lines: **Not included in the page limit.** **Included in the page limit.** | When they appear under a heading in the Build Your Application section. |
| Methods, if numbered. Like: **Method 1 — Approved rate** | In the Indirect costs section. |
| Name of agency contacts, or the first line in an address, like: **Delores Winderbatch**<br>Office of Dedicated Cats (ODC)<br>735 Independence Avenue<br>Government Way, DC | Anywhere they appear when text is set apart and could use visual hierarchy, like in the Contacts and Support section. |

#### Italics

Use sparingly, if at all.

You may use italics for:

- Titles of certain works in citations, if necessary.
- Certain legal or Latin terms (like *de minimis*) if necessary.

Italics can be hard to read, so use them with caution:

- People with dyslexia and other reading differences may find it difficult to read italicized content, particularly for long passages of text. Use plain text instead for passages or quotations.
- Don’t use italics for decorative or design purposes.

#### Underline

Don’t underline text, as users will often interpret it as a link.

For more information on creating links to internal sections of your document and links to external sites, see [Links](#links).

#### Other formatting

Avoid other Microsoft Word formatting like “Emphasis A” and “Emphasis B” as the design tool can’t read it.

Instead:

- Turn it into plain text.
- Use a sequential heading (H2 through H6 for most NOFOs) if it’s more appropriate for the context. Note that there should be no heading higher than H6.

### Capitalization

#### Title and heading capitalization

- For H1 only, use title case. Example: Step 1: Review the Opportunity.
- For all other headings, use sentence case. Example: Funding details.

See also [headings](#headings).

#### Tables, labels, and terms capitalization

- Use sentence case for table titles and labels.
- Keep terms and names lowercase unless they are part of a proper noun.

| Avoid | Use |
| --- | --- |
| Include your Resume and Work Plan | Include your resume and work plan |
| Get Federal assistance | Get federal assistance |
| The Federal government | The federal government |
| The federal reserve | The Federal Reserve |
| Any Tribal coalition | Any tribal coalition |

Note: We use lowercase for “tribe” and “tribal” in keeping with the approach of the Administration for Native Americans (ANA) within ACF, as well as the [style guide of the Indian Health Service [PDF]](https://www.ihs.gov/sites/webservices/themes/responsive2017/display_objects/documents/IHSWebsiteStyleGuide.pdf).

#### Internal link label capitalization

Follow the same conventions as the heading capitalization. Note that the links in this section are non-functional; they are meant only as a model to show how to create link text.

- Use title case when you internally link to an H1: Go to Get Ready to Apply.
- Use lowercase when linking to an H2 through H6: View funding policies and limitations.
- If necessary, rewrite for clarity when using internal links. Find the application package.

Tip: Command + Option + C toggles between sentence case, title case, and all caps, for heading fixes.

### Headings

- Use structured style headings for accessibility.
- For most NOFOs, use H2 through H6 only.
- For HRSA NOFOs, use H2 through H5 only.
- Do not use H7 or higher in any NOFOs, because it cannot be made accessible in a PDF.
- H1 uses Title Case formatting.
- H2 through H6 uses sentence case.
- Use headings sequentially to maintain a logical structure. For example, the next heading under an H2 needs to be an H3.
- Do not skip heading levels, such as jumping from an H1 directly to H3 without an H2 in between.

See also [capitalization](#capitalization).

### Lists

Consider a list when you need to break up long chunks of information.

But be mindful not to overuse them. If you have too many lists, they can lose effectiveness.

We generally follow [HHS guidance on lists](https://www.hhs.gov/digital/style-guide/index.html).

#### Bulleted lists

- Use standard bullet symbols (• and ◦).
- Capitalize the first letter of each bullet.
- Use a parallel structure, rather than a mix of tenses. We recommend following a consistent pattern (as in the example below) to make your list easier to read.
- Use a period at the end of most bulleted list items.
  - You don’t need periods if your list contains just a word or two (although they’re acceptable).
- If a bulleted section is complex and uses both fragments and full sentences — or two sentences in one — consider how to break apart for simplicity.

| Instead of | Try |
| --- | --- |
| A data management plan that includes:<ul><li>Data you will collect or generate</li><li>Archival and long-term data preservation plans</li><li>How you will update the Data Management Plan (DMP) as new information is available over the life of the project. You will provide updates to the DMP in annual reports.</li></ul> | Your data management plan should include:<ul><li>Data you will collect or generate.</li><li>Your archival and long-term data preservation plans.</li><li>How you’ll make updates when new information is available.</li></ul>You’ll provide updates to your plan in annual reports. |

- Delete semicolons at the end of bullets and connecting words (such as “and” or “or”). Instead, clarify in the lead-in text.

#### Numbered lists

Use a numbered list only when you need to preserve a chronological order or show you the order of steps in a process. Most lists will be bulleted lists.

#### Alphabetical lists

In general, don’t use this approach (labeling a list of items with a, b, c, etc.)

Instead of repeating the text of a law verbatim, where you might use alphabetical references, we recommend linking to the law directly and using plain language in the NOFO itself. See [legal references](#legal-references).

> **Tip:** Lists can get very messy in Word, especially with multiple people editing.
>
> - If you have a complicated list with multiple nested bullets, erase the formatting on the list and recreate the bullets.
> - Better yet, avoid complicated lists if you can. If a list is complex, consider another way to display the information.

### Punctuation

#### Ampersands (&)

In general, don’t use the ampersand symbol. Instead, replace it by using the word “and.” The only exceptions are if the ampersand is:

- A required part of a formal name.
- Necessary due to space constraints.

#### Dashes and hyphens

Avoid using dashes of any kind for date and time ranges.

Instead, use the word “to.”

| Don’t | Do |
| --- | --- |
| 8:30 a.m.–9:30 a.m. ET | 8:30 to 9:30 a.m. ET |
| From 8:15 a.m.-noon ET | 8:15 a.m. to noon ET |
| 2026–2027 | 2026 to 2027 |

#### Long dashes, or em-dashes [—]

Avoid use of the long dash, or em-dash, whenever possible. Although it’s grammatically correct to use it in the following examples, it’s usually a sign that your sentence is too complex and that you should rewrite it.

Avoiding complex sentences improves comprehension for everyone, including people under time pressure.

| Instead of this | Try this |
| --- | --- |
| There was something about this NOFO — Gerald was sure of it — that was really special. | Gerald was sure there was something special about this NOFO. |
| In addition to the specialty requirements — a letter of commitment, your financial disclosure, and member list — you need to include your proof of eligibility. | Include your:<ul><li>Letter of commitment.</li><li>Financial disclosure.</li><li>Member list.</li><li>Proof of eligibility.</li></ul> |
| Your director cannot also be the investigator — even if they were the investigator last year — due to new regulations. | Your director cannot be the investigator on your project. |

#### Hyphens

Words with hyphens can take longer to scan, and you can usually rewrite a sentence to avoid using a hyphen altogether.

| Instead of this | Try this |
| --- | --- |
| This is a 400-year-old NOFO. | This NOFO is 400 years old. |

In general, omit a hyphen in certain words [PDF] when there is no ambiguity after leaving it out.

| Instead of this | Try this |
| --- | --- |
| sub-sector | subsector |
| boiler-plate | boilerplate |
| non-profit | nonprofit |

However, there are times when a hyphen is necessary. For example, the following sentences have different meanings:

- Frankenstein’s monster loved re-creation.
- Frankenstein’s monster loved recreation.
- We recovered the book about NOFOs.
- We re-covered the book about NOFOs.

Also consider where you might rephrase your sentence. In the final sentence, for example, you could also just say, “We put a new cover on the NOFO book.”

#### Oxford comma

Use it!

**Example:** We want to buy bananas, grapefruit, birdseed, and milk today.

#### Percent

Use the % symbol instead of the word “percent,” as screen readers are able to recognize the symbol. See the [HHS style guide](https://www.hhs.gov/digital/style-guide/index.html).

## Numbers

### General guidance

This guidance follows the [HHS style guide](https://www.hhs.gov/digital/style-guide/index.html).

- Spell out all numbers under 10.
- Use numerals for all numbers above nine.
- Use numerals for all ages, sums of money, and times of day.
- Do not use Roman numerals (IV, V, VI).

| Don’t | Do |
| --- | --- |
| Submit twelve attachments | Submit 12 attachments. |
| Children up to seven years old | Children age 7 and younger. |

It’s best to leave space around numbers. They can also be hard to read if they’re grouped closely together with other numbers or words (in a sentence, for example).

Instead, separate numbers so they’re easier to scan.

Get more information about presenting numbers at Accessible Numbers.

Credit to UK researcher and content designer Laura Parker, who has lived experience with low numeracy.

### Dates and time

Use this format for dates: February 1, 2027

For time:

- Write out times, using a.m. and p.m. with periods (ACF).
- Spell out noon and midnight.
- Use “to” instead of the en-dash [–].
- If the time is on the hour, the “:00” is not required.
- Always use ET and not EST.
- When expressing a range, you don’t need to repeat a.m. or p.m. with each number.

| 🚫 Instead of | ✅ Do this |
| --- | --- |
| 8:00 A.M. to 5:30 P.M. EST | 8 a.m. to 5:30 p.m. ET |
| 9AM to 11AM | 9 to 11 a.m. ET |
| 8 a.m. to 12:00 pm | 8 a.m. to noon ET |

### Phone numbers

Use this format: 555-555-1212.

Breaking apart numbers into smaller “chunks” can make them easier to recognize and use.

## Document structure and components

### Appendices

To add an appendix to your NOFO, follow these guidelines:

- Include it in the same file as the NOFO.
- Put it at the very end.
- Use proper heading structure:
  - H1 if you only have one appendix
  - If you have more than one appendix:
    - Create an H1 title called “Appendices.”
    - Use H2 for each actual Appendix listed afterward.
    - For example: Appendix A is H2, Appendix B is H2, Appendix C is H2, etc.

### Callout boxes

You can add callout boxes anywhere in the text.

- Add a table with one cell to create a callout box.
- Use normal text formatting.
- If you want the callout box to have a heading, add it as the first element.

### Default gray text

Don’t make edits to gray text. This text standard across NOFO templates and the language has been negotiated by HHS.

You can ask HHS for approval to make bigger changes to gray text.

### Document formatting

- Don’t use manual page breaks.
- Don’t use section marks.
- Before you send on a NOFO to design, make sure to delete all comments (not just resolve them).

### File naming conventions

<!-- The source's table of contents says "File naming conventions"; its section heading says "File and naming conventions". -->

Note: Writing and editing process may vary across teams. We recommend having only one active file and using naming conventions to make sure everyone does their part in the most recent and accurate document, and no one has to repeat any work.

#### Have only one active file at a time and archive the rest

- You should only have one active file at a time in the main folder for any NOFO.
- All previous versions of that file should be placed in a subfolder specifically titled “Archive” for that NOFO.
- When you move an outdated file to the archive, add “outdated” at the front of the file name and XxX at the end. This can help avoid version control mistakes.
  - Example: `Outdated-ACFORR_0020_RefugeeCareers_3.5.24_JH_forExecSeXxX.docx`

#### Use file naming conventions

- Remove previous stages from file names.
- If you have a “final” file that is later superseded, remove “final” from that filename and add a different stage.
- Archive the no-longer-active version by moving it to the Archive folder.

| File convention | Looks like |
| --- | --- |
| `Agency_Last4_KeyWordsFromTitle_Date_LastReviewer_Stage.docx` | `ACFORR_0020_RefugeeCareers_3.5.24_JH_forExecSec.docx`<br>`ACFORR_0020_RefugeeCareers_3.20.24_FinalApproved.docx`<br>`Outdated_ACFORR_0020_RefugeeCareers_3.20.24_TeamEdited_XxX.docx` |

### Footnotes and endnotes

- You must use the Microsoft Word tool for adding endnotes.
- If you have multiple references to the same source throughout your document, each reference to that source must be given its own unique citation. Do not add endnotes manually in place of using the Word tool.
- Although you will not be including in-parenthetical citations, you’ll otherwise format your endnote sources using APA style.
- Do not manually add a, “Endnotes” heading at the end of your NOFO. An “Endnotes” heading is generated automatically if there are any footnotes/endnotes in the document. If you manually create a heading, it will show up in duplicate in design.

### Glossary

If you’re considering a glossary, ask yourself whether it’s necessary — will people use it?

If you have a small number of specialized words you need to define more clearly, consider placing them in a callout box near where they are referenced in your text. See [Callout boxes](#callout-boxes).

If you find that you do need a wider glossary for programmatic reasons, include it at the end of the NOFO.

Title it:

- “Appendix: Glossary” in H1.
- Or “Appendix [X]: Glossary” if you have more than one appendix to add in the appendices section.

For more information, see [Appendices](#appendices).

### Informational calls and webinars

#### Enter your call details

Present webinar information clearly. Enter dates, times, and other important information separately (see below) rather than embedding them in sentence text. This is guided by research on how people comprehend numbers.

List full details for your informational call or webinar in the “Join the webinar” section in Step 2: Get ready to apply, following the Find the application package section. You should also include the webinar date in “Key dates” under Basic information in Step 1: Review the Opportunity.

#### Example of informational call information

Your language may vary slightly:

> **Join the webinar (or informational call)**
>
> For more information about this opportunity, join our webinar.
>
> - Link to join: [Join the webinar on Teams].
> - Date: Monday, February 1, 2027.
> - Time: 2 to 3:30 p.m. ET.
>
> If you cannot join through your computer, you can call in.
>
> - Phone number: 555-849-3275.
> - Meeting ID: 160 109 0193.
> - Passcode: 4066 5987.
>
> We will record the webinar. If you cannot join live, you can replay it at the Grants section of our website. [Note: The link text in this example is for illustration only and is not meant to be an active link.]

| Join by phone | Meeting ID | Passcode |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 833-568-8864 | 160 109 0193 | 4066 5987 |

#### How to format call details

Break the meeting ID number into chunks so it’s easier to read.

| No | Yes |
| --- | --- |
| Meeting ID 1601090193 | Meeting ID 160 109 0193 |

Do not use an exposed URL as a link. Instead, use clearly written text as your link.

| No | Yes |
| --- | --- |
| Join the webinar on Teams: `https://teams.microsoft.com/l/meetup-join/19%3ameeting_NzA1Y2JmOTEt…` (a very long exposed URL) | Join the webinar in Teams. |

Don’t add room system information unless your program specifies it.

Teams may choose to set up their own registration page for an informational call or webinar. In that case, it’s not necessary to include complete webinar information in the NOFO if applicants can find it on the registration page. Include:

- Date and time of the call.
- Clear link to the registration page with link text that says <Register for the [webinar or] informational call>.
- Do not paste the exposed URL.

### Legal references

#### Code of Federal Regulations (CFR)

- Do not begin a paragraph with a CFR reference. Include it at the end of the sentence instead.
- Use lowercase for “part” and “subpart.”
- Include a link to the specific CFR.
- Do not include the section mark (§). Symbols are not universally machine-readable and may be hard for humans to read as well.
- Use the [Code of Federal Regulations](https://www.ecfr.gov/) as your source for CFR references.
- View the instructions on inserting CFR references.

#### Statutes

Link to:

- The law’s site, if it has one, such as the [Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA)](https://sites.ed.gov/idea/).
- Or use one of the following:
  - [GovInfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/).
  - [U.S. Code](https://uscode.house.gov/).

#### Executive orders

Use whichever site your agency typically uses. Otherwise use one of the following:

- [Federal Register](https://www.federalregister.gov/).
- [White House](https://www.whitehouse.gov/).

### Links

Make link text descriptive and meaningful.

- Link text (“labels”) should always be clear even when out of context from the rest of a sentence or paragraph.
- Labels that say “click here,” “here,” or “read more” are repetitive, unclear, and can create accessibility issues.
- When writing text for a link, be specific: What will someone see when they follow the link? The user should be able to understand from the link or button text what the call to action is (the purpose) and where they will end up if they click on the link (the target).

| Don’t | Do |
| --- | --- |
| For more information, \<click here\> | \<Read the federal policy.\> |
| Check it out \<here\> | \<Search for a grant.\> |
| Select \<this link\> | \<View lab results.\> |
| \<Take a look\> | \<Make an appointment\>. |
| | \<Read the report [PDF].\> |

- If you have multiple links that use the same link language, they should take the user to the same place. Otherwise change your wording.
- Use 100 characters or less in any link label.
- If you are linking to a PDF, include “[PDF]” as part of the link itself. Example: If you experience a systems issue with Grants.gov or SAM.gov, please refer to [ACF’s Policy for Applicants Experiencing Federal Systems Issues [PDF]](https://www.hhs.gov/guidance/sites/default/files/hhs-guidance-documents//systems_issue_policy_final.pdf).

#### Internal links

- For users of the browser version (Microsoft Word for the web).
- For users of the Microsoft Desktop App.

We recommend adding internal links in the browser version because it’s easier to check that the link is working correctly. However, you usually are not able to change link labels in the browser version once you’ve inserted the link. Instead, you have to delete the text with the link and add it back in. Conversely, when using the Desktop App, it’s harder to check your link locations, but easier to edit text.

##### Browser version (Microsoft Word for the web)

1. **Make sure your headings are styled as headings.**
   Your headings can’t just look like headings, for example, by bolding the text. Instead, they have to use one of the built-in Word styles. Otherwise, Word will not recognize them as headings, and you will not be able to link to them.

   To check the style of a piece of text while using Microsoft Word for the web, click on or highlight the text. Then click on the Home tab and look for the box that shows the style applied to the selected text. In this example, the Summary heading is correctly tagged as Heading 3, also referred to as H3.

   Note: While Word may allow you to create internal links using bookmarks, we do not use that method because it can delay the design and production process. Make sure you link using headings only.
2. **Find the text you want to use as your link.**
   Highlight that text with your cursor to select it.
3. **Go to the Insert toolbar and find the Link button.**
   Click on the Insert toolbar and select the dropdown symbol next to Link and go to the Insert Link button.
4. **Select the “This Document” tab.**
   In the Link menu, select the “This Document” tab and scroll down to the heading you want to link to. You will see a list of headings that reflects the hierarchy of your document and the text you selected as your link will appear in the “Display text:” box. Click the “Insert” button to create the link.
5. **Check to see that your link was inserted correctly.**
   In the browser version of Word, when you click on a link, a pop-up will appear showing the heading the link points to. Make sure it’s correct and then confirm that the link text is correct as well.

##### Microsoft Word desktop app

1. **Make sure your headings are styled as headings.**
   Your headings can’t just look like headings, for example, by bolding the text. Instead, they have to use one of the built-in Word styles. Otherwise, Word will not recognize them as headings, and you will not be able to link to them.

   To check the style of a piece of text while using the Word desktop app, click on or highlight the text. Select the Styles pane in the Home tab, and a panel will appear, showing the current style assigned to your text. You can also use the smaller Styles section on the Home tab, but the pane is easier to use. In this example, the Summary heading is correctly tagged as Heading 3, also referred to as H3.

   Note: While Word may allow you to create internal links using bookmarks, we do not use that method because it can delay the design and production process. Make sure you link using headings only.
2. **Find the text you want to use as your link.**
   Highlight that text with your cursor to select it.
3. **Go to the Insert menu and find the Hyperlink button.**
   Click on the Insert menu and scroll down to Hyperlink and select it.
4. **Select the “This Document” tab.**
   In the Insert Hyperlink menu, select the “This Document” tab and scroll down to the heading you want to link to. You will see a list of headings that reflects the hierarchy of your document and the text you selected as your link will appear in the “Text to Display:” box. You can expand any heading that has a triangle next to it to see additional headings you can link to. Click “OK” to create the link.
5. **Check to see that your link was inserted correctly.**
   In the Desktop app, the only way to test a link is to follow it. Click on the link and confirm that it goes to the correct location in your document and then confirm that the link text is correct as well.

### Tables

Give every table a caption — a short label naming what it shows, placed directly above the table. A caption can be either:

- A descriptive heading directly above the table.
- A paragraph in bold, normal-size text, using the format "Table: Title of table."

Use a "Table:" caption when a table isn't close to its heading, such as when there's a paragraph or two of text in between, or when the heading itself doesn't clearly describe the table's contents. Every table needs a caption in one of these forms.

Note: This section was clarified from the source edition to say that a descriptive heading can serve as a table's caption. See [Changes from the source edition](#changes-from-the-source-edition).

- Keep captions in sentence case.

Note: A caption can be separated from its table by up to one paragraph, but no more.

The following tables don't require a "Table:" caption, because a heading directly precedes them and already provides context:

- Narratives.
- Attachments.
- Other required forms.
- Project narrative.
- Scoring criteria tables for subsections of the Project narrative.
- Application checklist.
- Research plan sections.

#### Simple vs. complex tables

When possible, try to break complex tables into a few simpler tables or flatten out the header cells. This makes them more accessible and easier for people to follow.

A complex table has merged cells, multiple levels of row or column headers, or other structural relationships that make it harder to associate data cells with the correct headers.

A simple table has a clear structure where each data cell can be easily associated with its row or column header.

### Taglines and metadata

#### Taglines

A tagline is required in the Basic information section, in Step 1: Review the Opportunity. It’s a very short, high-level statement of the overall goal of the NOFO. Think of it as a phrase that clearly explains what your NOFO will accomplish, without all the detail.

- Use sentence case.
- Keep “Tagline:” first so the design tool will pick it up correctly.
- Add a period at the end of the tagline.
- Suggested length: 15 words or fewer.

**Examples**

- Tagline: Improving health outcomes for people with asthma by expanding access to community health services.
- Tagline: Supporting rural health research under the Rural Health Research Dissemination Program to inform policy at national, state, and local levels.
- Tagline: Creating a network of the Nation’s Children’s Hospitals that collaborates with their communities to prepare and respond to pediatric needs during disasters and national emergencies.

#### Metadata subjects

Metadata subjects, keywords, and authors help categorize and make content more searchable, especially in digital systems.

- Use a broad, formulaic one-liner statement that expresses the purpose of the NOFO.
- Suggested length: ~ 25 words or fewer.

To write the metadata subject line for a NOFO, you can follow this formula:

> A notice of funding opportunity from the [Agency or OpDiv] to [the purpose of the NOFO].

**Examples**

- Metadata subject: A notice of funding opportunity from the Health Resources and Services Administration to fund technical assistance providers to improve mental health and engagement in care among people with HIV.
- Metadata subject: A notice of funding opportunity from the Office of Family Assistance on providing healthy marriage and parenting skills education to youth.

#### Metadata keywords

To choose your keywords, think about what words you’d put into a search bar to look for this specific NOFO.

- Use specific terms or phrases that come directly from the language of the NOFO.
- Address the more specific details of the NOFO.
- Separate terms with commas.
- Suggested length: 8-10 keywords.

**Examples**

- Metadata keywords: Administration for Children and Families, Office of Family Assistance, READY4Life, youth, parenting youth, healthy marriage and relationship education, pregnant youth
- Metadata keywords: Application guide, HRSA HIV/AIDS Bureau, mental health, people with HIV, notice of funding opportunity, HIV and mental health, mental health care

#### Metadata author

- Use the name of the Agency or OpDiv funding the NOFO.
- Spell out the full name, followed by the acronym in parentheses.

**Examples**

- Metadata author: Administration for Children and Families (ACF)
- Metadata author: Health Resources and Services Administration (HRSA)

## Language

### Contractions

#### Use simple positive contractions

They can help your text sound clear and conversational.

Examples include: we’ll, it’s, you'll, we're, they're, and there's.

#### Avoid negative or conditional contractions

People can find them hard to read and may misunderstand them.

Examples of contractions to avoid: won’t, shouldn't, can’t, and wouldn’t.

### Inclusive language

Be thoughtful with your word choice. View the preferred term list from the CDC.

### Word choice and phrasing

**and, or**
Avoid and/or and similar uses of the forward slash.

- Instead, just choose one (e.g., “and” or “or”).
- Or try:
  - Birds or rabbits, or both.
  - Apples, oranges, or both.
  - Either judges or clerks.

**applicant**
Avoid this when referring to what applicants must do in your NOFO. Talk with them directly by using “you.”

Also avoid the use of (s), as in applicant(s). Make it singular or plural. Or, when needed, say “one or more applicants.”

**child care**
Not childcare or child-care.

**directional language (“above,” “below”)**
Don’t use directional language in your NOFO document, like “see below for…” or “referenced above…” It creates accessibility issues. Instead, link directly to that section or try these alternatives:

- For “below”:
  - “in the following list.”
  - “In the table that follows.”
  - “in the next section.”
- For “above”:
  - “in the previous section.”

**federal**
Lowercase, unless you are referring to the Federal Register or another proper noun.

**nonprofit**
Not non-profit or not-for-profit.

**notice of award, award notice**
For plain language, consider saying, “award notice” or “notice of award,” instead of using the acronym NoA.

**notice of funding opportunity**
In plain text, consider where you can say “this funding opportunity,” instead of “NOFO.”

**work plan**
Use lowercase as two words. Not workplan or Work Plan.
