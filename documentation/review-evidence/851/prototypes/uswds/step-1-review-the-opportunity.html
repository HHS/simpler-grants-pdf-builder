Summary

Link to this section: Summary

We know it can be challenging for states to fund the work necessary to improve the Essential Health Benefits (EHB). Improving EHB typically involves:

  • Comprehensively reviewing EHB-benchmark plans.
  • Seeking public feedback on the coverage of EHB in the state.
  • Submitting an application to CMS to change the EHB in the state.

We understand that, due in part to these challenges, that there are many states that have not considered updating their EHB-benchmark plans. These states include 24 of the 28 states expected to have a Federally-facilitated Exchange (FFE) as of January 1, 2026.

As a result, the covered benefits in the Qualified Health Plans (QHPs) offered through the Exchanges in these states may not reflect recent changes in medical evidence, scientific advancement, and clinical care guidelines. Additionally, people enrolled in these QHPs may have trouble accessing EHB because of cost, claims denials, utilization management, discriminatory benefit design, or other factors.

We want to lessen these challenges through the EHB-Benchmark Plan Modernization Grant for States with a Federally-facilitated Exchange (FFE EHB Mod Grant). This new grant program will fund states with an FFE for a variety of activities related to the review of EHB-benchmark plans. The goal is to help applicants modernize and change their EHB-benchmark plans under 45 CFR 156.111.

Program description

Link to this section: Program description

Purpose

Link to this section: Purpose

The purpose of this funding opportunity is to provide funding to eligible states with an FFE to review and potentially change their EHB-benchmark plans because of:

  • Enrollees experiencing difficulties in accessing needed services.
  • Changes in medical evidence or scientific advancement
  • Changes in the health needs of their populations.
  • Other clinical considerations as authorized by the Affordable Care Act (ACA).

Background

Link to this section: Background

Section 1301(a)(1)(B) of the ACA directs all QHP issuers to cover the EHB package described in section 1302(a) of the ACA. The EHB package includes:

  • Coverage of the services described in section 1302(b) of the ACA.
  • Adherence to the cost-sharing limits described in section 1302(c) of the ACA
  • Meeting the actuarial value levels established in section 1302(d) of the ACA.

Section 2707(a) of the PHS Act, which is effective for plan or policy years beginning on or after January 1, 2014, extends the requirement to cover the EHB package to non-grandfathered individual and small group health insurance coverage, whether that coverage is offered through an Exchange. Section 1302(b) of the ACA requires the Secretary of HHS to define the EHB and make sure that the EHB:

  • Includes at least the following general categories and the items and services covered within the categories:
    • Ambulatory patient services.
    • Emergency services.
    • Hospitalization.
    • Maternity and newborn care.
    • Mental health and substance use disorder services.
    • Including behavioral health treatment.
    • Prescription drugs.
    • Rehabilitative and habilitative services and devices.
    • Laboratory services.
    • Preventive and wellness services and chronic disease management.
    • Pediatric services, including oral and vision care.
  • Is equal in scope to the benefits provided under a typical employer plan.
  • Reflect an appropriate balance among the categories, so that benefits are not unduly weighted toward any category.
  • Does not make coverage decisions, determine reimbursement rates, establish incentive programs, or design benefits in ways that discriminate against individuals because of their age, disability, or expected length of life.
  • Takes into account the health care needs of diverse populations, including women, children, persons with disabilities, and other groups.
  • Makes sure that individuals are not denied essential health benefits on the basis of their age, expected length of life, present or predicted disability, degree of medical dependency, or quality of life.
  • Is periodically reviewed and updated to address any gaps in access to coverage, changes in medical evidence, or scientific advancement.

CMS defined the EHB using a benchmark-based approach in the February 25, 2013 Federal Register (78 FR 12834) (EHB Rule). Under this approach, a health plan provides the EHB if it provides, among other things:

  • Benefits that are substantially equal to the EHB-benchmark plan including covered benefits.
  • Limitations on coverage (including coverage of benefit amount, duration, and scope) that are substantially equal to the EHB-benchmark plan.
  • Prescription drug benefits that meet the requirements of 45 CFR 156.122.

Thus, a state’s EHB-benchmark plan serves as a reference plan for benefits defined as EHB in the state.

States play an integral role in determining what benefits are included in EHB-benchmark plans. For plan years beginning on or after January 1, 2020, states select or change an EHB-benchmark plan by meeting the requirements at 45 CFR 156.111, which include certain actuarial analysis and confirmations that the state’s selected EHB-benchmark plan meets the scope of benefits requirements at § 156.111(b)(2).

CMS recently revised the requirements at § 156.111 in the April 15, 2024 Federal Register (89 FR 26218) (2025 Payment Notice), to make this process easier to understand and to undertake, with the ultimate goal of promoting more frequent EHB-benchmark plan changes to expand coverage. Twelve states, including only four states with an FFE (Alaska, Michigan, North Dakota, and South Dakota), have used 156.111 to add or expand coverage for certain benefits to EHB-benchmark plans since 2020.[3]

Program requirements and expectations

Link to this section: Program requirements and expectations

At a minimum, you must use grant funds to perform the following activities. You may engage contractors such as actuarial, health policy, or clinical firms to help with all or some of these activities, under your guidance and direction.

You must do all of the following:

  1. Undertake a comprehensive review of the state’s current EHB-benchmark plan and consider potential changes based on your review. At a minimum, you must:
  2. Identify whether enrollees are having trouble accessing the benefits in the state’s EHB-benchmark plan for reasons of cost, claims denials, utilization management, discriminatory benefit design, or other factors. You may fulfill this analysis through statistically valid consumer surveys, engaging in outreach with interested parties such as consumer groups, and reviewing data on issuer claim denials, utilization management, or consumer complaints related to consumers enrolled in the FFE.
  3. Ensure health equity. To ensure that EHB-benchmark plans are designed to further health equity[4] initiatives and address health care disparities, your analysis must address how the state’s EHB-benchmark plan could be updated to better meet the needs of people:
  4. With lower incomes.
  5. Of all ages, including children and older adults.
  6. With disabilities or who have a greater risk of developing serious health issues.
  7. Of color.
  8. Who identify as LGBTQIA+.
  9. In rural communities.
  10. Who have limited English proficiency.
  11. Who have been historically underserved, marginalized, and adversely affected by persistent poverty and inequality.
  12. Identify areas where the state’s EHB-benchmark plan could be updated to account for changes in medical evidence, scientific advancement, and clinical care guidelines. This may involve consulting or contracting with one or more clinical experts to analyze the state’s EHB-benchmark plan,

For example, states could do one or more of the following:

  • Consider whether benefit visit and other limits are clinically based and meet the needs of a diverse population, or whether they are arbitrary.
  • Look at current EHB-benchmark coverage for particular conditions or scenarios, such as HIV, diabetes, infertility, chronic kidney disease, or developmental disabilities, and compare that coverage to generally accepted clinical guidelines.
  • Examine whether coverage prevents access to medical advancements such as new radiology and laboratory methods, cost-effective yet newer advancements such as silver diamine fluoride, or newer treatment modalities such as doula care and peer recovery support services.
  • Identify areas where the state’s EHB-benchmark plan could be updated to account for national and/or state healthcare concerns.
    • You must examine your EHB-benchmark plan for how it may account for nationwide health care concerns (such as the opioid epidemic or addressing mental health concerns).
    • You must also examine your EHB-benchmark plan for how it accounts for concerns that may be specific to the state (such as diabetes, obesity, or maternal health outcomes).
    • You must identify at least five areas where your EHB-benchmark plan may need to be modified or updated to account for a national healthcare concern, a state healthcare concern, or both.
  • Perform a regional comparison analysis. You must assess how the state’s EHB-benchmark plan’s benefits compare to at least three other state EHB-benchmark plans in a nearby geographic region. This assessment must include whether there are differences in the scope of EHB (such as maternity and newborn care), benefits not covered as EHB for which there is a public health need or expectation of coverage (such as durable medical equipment), or other deficiencies.
  • Ensure the state’s EHB-benchmark plan is clear and unambiguous. You must perform a comprehensive review of its EHB-benchmark plan to determine whether it is sufficiently clear which benefits are covered and the scope of benefits. If the plan is ambiguous in any way, you must consider how to make it clearer.
  • Submit a Section 508-compliant report to CMS for publication and publish the same report on a public state website. The report must reflect the findings of your comprehensive review of your current EHB-benchmark plan, as described in the previous requirements.
  • For applicants that decide to submit pursuant to the process at 156.111(b)(2)(ii) before the end of the period of performance: Submit such an application to CMS by May 6, 2026, for effectiveness in plan year 2028, or by May 5, 2027, for effectiveness in plan year 2029. We encourage you to submit an application to update your EHB-benchmark plan after the end of the award period, but the grant will not be extended to provide you with support. We urge you to apply to update your EHB-benchmark plans as a result of completing the requirements of this grant, but this application is not required. States that decide to submit an EHB-benchmark plan submission before the end of the period of performance may use grant funds to conduct the required actuarial analysis to demonstrate that the state’s new EHB-benchmark plan complies with the typicality regulatory requirements at 156.111(b)(2).[5] You do not have to include your EHB-benchmark plan application in the report described in program requirement #2.
  • For applicants that decide to seek additional grant funds for an advisory board to advise the state: Applicants that create advisory boards may not require the advisory board to be responsible for completing other program requirements. At a minimum, a state’s advisory board must:
  • Include patients and patient representatives on the advisory board.
  • Ensure the participation or perspective of persons:
    • With lower incomes.
    • Of all ages, including children and older adults.
    • With disabilities or who have a greater risk of developing serious health issues.
    • Of color.
    • Who identify as LGBTQIA+.
    • In rural communities.
    • Who have limited English proficiency.
    • Who have been historically underserved, marginalized, and adversely affected by persistent poverty and inequality.
  • Be subject to a public notice and comment process to address updates and full public transparency on advisory board proceedings and materials.
  • Be subject to disclosure and conflict of interest protocols.

Limitations
Link to this section: Limitations

We do not allow the following costs:

  • Pre-award costs.
  • Meeting matching requirements for any other federal funds or local entities.
  • Services, equipment, or supports that are the legal responsibility of another party under federal, state, or tribal law, such as vocational rehabilitation or education services, or under any civil rights laws, such as modifying a workplace or providing accommodations that are obligations under law.
  • Goods or services not allocable to the project.
  • Supplanting existing state, local, tribal, or private funding of infrastructure or services, such as staff salaries, etc.
  • Construction.
  • Capital expenditures for improvements to land, buildings, or equipment that materially increase their value or useful life as a direct cost, except with our prior written approval.
  • The cost of independent research and development, including their proportionate share of indirect costs (see 45 CFR 75.476).
  • Funds related to any activity designed to influence the enactment of legislation, appropriations, regulation, administrative action, or executive order.
  • Certain telecommunications and video surveillance equipment (see 2 CFR 200.216).
  • Meals, unless in limited circumstances such as:
    • Subjects and patients under study.
    • Where specifically approved as part of the project or program activity, such as in programs providing children’s services.
    • As part of a per diem or subsistence allowance provided in conjunction with allowable travel.
  • Costs that supplant funds for any other Notice of Funding Opportunity or Financial Assistance Program. You cannot use grant funds for the same grant activities for which you receive other federal grant dollars (for example, grant activities performed under the Expanding Access to Women’s Health Grant). If you receive other federal grant dollars for these grant activities, you must explain in your budget narrative how other federal grant dollars are not used to perform grant activities performed under this grant.
  • Other than for normal and recognized executive-legislative relationships or participation by an agency or officer of a state, local, or tribal government in policymaking and administrative processes within the executive branch of that government, funding awarded under this NOFO may not be used for:
    • Paying the salary or expenses of any grant recipient, or agent acting for such recipient, related to any activity designed to influence the enactment of legislation, appropriations, regulation, administrative action, or executive order proposed or pending before the Congress or any state government, state legislature, or local legislature or legislative body.
    • Lobbying, but awardees can lobby at their own expense if they can segregate federal funds from other financial resources used for lobbying.
Program-specific limitations
Link to this section: Program-specific limitations

You must also follow these program-specific funding limitations:

  • Funds requested in your budget must be derived from the approximate enrollment size of the off-Exchange market relative to the on-Exchange market in the states eligible to apply for this grant.
  • You cannot use grant funds to account for a percentage of program requirements that impact enrollees in off-Exchange plans in the non-grandfathered individual and small group markets.
  • On average, total enrollment in the FFEs accounts for approximately 80% of total enrollment in non-grandfathered individual and small group health insurance coverage in states with an FFE.
    • You are responsible for the remaining percentage of costs to perform program requirements. You must explain in your budget narrative how you plan to account for this proportion of costs to ensure that user fees funds are not used to impact non-grandfathered individual and small group health insurance coverage in the off-Exchange market in your state.
    • Specifically address how these costs will not be funded by user fees.
    • State employee salary hours (such as an actuary) may be used as non-federal share if grants funds are not supplanted.
    • Include the date of your most recently approved cost allocation plan.
Indirect costs
Link to this section: Indirect costs

Indirect costs are those for a common or joint purpose across more than one project and that cannot be easily separated by project.

To charge indirect costs you can select one of two methods:

Method 1 – Approved rate. You currently have an indirect cost rate or cost allocation plan approved by your cognizant federal agency.

Method 2 – De minimis rate. Per 2 CFR 200.414(f), if you have never received a negotiated indirect cost rate, you may elect to charge a de minimis rate. If you are awaiting approval of an indirect cost proposal you may also use the de minimis rate. If you choose this method, costs included in the indirect cost pool must not be charged as direct costs.

This rate is 15% of modified total direct costs (MTDC). See 2 CFR 200.1 for the definition of MTDC. You can use this rate indefinitely.

Statutory authority

Link to this section: Statutory authority

Section 1311(d)(5)(A) of the ACA allows an Exchange to charge user fees to issuers of QHPs, “to support [the Exchange’s] operations.” CMS, as operator of the FFE in 28 states that have not elected to establish their own State-based Exchange, assesses user fees to issuers that offer QHPs on an FFE. In addition, section 1311(e)(1)(B) of the ACA also requires an Exchange to ensure that health plans available through the Exchange are in the interests of qualified individuals and qualified employers in the state.
